## The brief

Kathleen Renders runs a dessert and catering business and was managing
orders, workshop registrations and shopping lists by hand. Skills
Integration Lab paired her with our team of five as a real client — she
set the requirements, we ran the sprints, and she used what we delivered.

The result is a Laravel application where customers order desserts, claim
discounted surplus stock, register for workshops and submit catering
requests, and where the owner manages the entire catalogue and reads
sales analytics.

Working with a non-technical client changed how requirements arrived.
"I want to know what I need to buy this week" is not a specification —
turning it into the shopping-list generator meant asking what she
actually did on a Monday morning, not what she thought a system should
do.

## What I built

The project was split across the team; these are the parts I owned.

**Catering requests, end to end.** Customers submit a request with event
type, date, guest count, budget and a package. They can pick specific
desserts as preferences, seeing portion sizes and package surcharges as
they choose. Themes come from a list or are typed in freely and added on
the fly — the client's business genuinely has an open set of themes, and
forcing a fixed dropdown would have made the feature useless to her. The
whole confirmation mail flow is automated from the submitted request.

**Ingredient management.** A full CRUD system for ingredients, including
the logic that links allergens to ingredients. Allergens propagate up:
because a dessert is made of ingredients, its allergen list is derived
rather than maintained separately, which means it cannot go stale when a
recipe changes.

**Admin analytics.** A price-evolution page for the admin dashboard, with
interactive Chart.js visualisations of price and order data over time,
and a toggle between chart and table views. The table view exists because
the client asked for it — she wanted the numbers, not only the shape.

**Login**, front end and backend.

**Codebase architecture.** Late in the project I restructured the folder
layout into meaningful domains — `Admin/Catalog`, `Frontend/Checkout` and
so on — and normalised naming conventions across routes, filenames and
Livewire components. Five people had each been naming things reasonably
and differently, and by then finding a component meant guessing which
convention its author had used.

## What working in a team taught me

Refactoring someone else's naming is a social problem before it is a
technical one. Doing it in one pass, with the reasoning written down and
agreed first, worked. Doing it gradually would have produced merge
conflicts and the impression I was quietly rewriting other people's work.

Scrum with Jira, Clockify time tracking and burndown charts was the first
time I saw estimation feedback: you commit to a sprint, you miss, and the
chart tells you exactly how wrong you were. That is a more useful
correction than any amount of advice about estimating.

## Stack

Laravel with Livewire and Blade, Alpine.js for client-side interaction,
Tailwind for styling, MySQL, Stripe for payments, and nightly automated
deployment to Combell through a GitHub Actions workflow.
