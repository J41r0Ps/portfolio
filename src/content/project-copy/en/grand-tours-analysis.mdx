## The problem

The graded version of this project covered one race, forbade Pandas, and
answered four questions from four disconnected files with no shared key.
It met the brief. It was also the wrong shape for the question I actually
wanted to ask: do the three Grand Tours behave like one sport, or three?

So I rebuilt it. Three races instead of one, a proper relational model, a
tested `src/` package, and the original preserved in `legacy/` as a
deliberate before-and-after.

## What I built

Two tidy tables replacing four ad-hoc files: `editions` (333 rows, one per
race-edition) and `riders` (161 rows, with per-race win counts), joined on
a shared key.

The three Wikipedia pages spell the same columns differently — `Sponsor/Team`,
`Sponsor / team`, `Sponsor/team`. Rather than indexing by position, I
normalise the headers and look them up in an alias table, so one function
handles all three races and survives Wikipedia reordering its columns.

Rider birth dates came from Wikidata over SPARQL in four batched requests,
replacing roughly a hundred individual page scrapes. Names returning two
distinct birth dates were excluded rather than guessed: picking one inserts
a plausible wrong value that nothing downstream can detect.

## The modelling decision that mattered most

Editions needed four states, not a valid/invalid flag: `held` (294),
`no_race` (23, the world wars), `disputed` (7, the annulled Tours of
1999–2005) and `team_only` (1).

Disputed editions have routes and distances, so they belong in
route-level analysis — but they have no winner to credit. A binary flag
forces a wrong answer to one of those two questions. Four states answer
both correctly.

## What the data says

Convergence is the recurring pattern. Distance went from over 2,000 km
apart in the 1920s to within roughly 200; speed from 4 km/h apart to
within 2 since 1980; national concentration from total dominance to a
similar spread everywhere.

But the races never moved *together*. Raw Giro–Tour distance correlation
is 0.565; after differencing to remove the shared century-long trend, it
falls to 0.160. That is independent drift toward a shared constraint —
UCI regulation — not coordination. Correlating two series that both
decline over a century tells you almost nothing until you difference them.

Winners have aged by 1.95 years across the whole history: p = 0.0008 by
permutation test, r² = 0.038. Real, measurable, and small. Both facts get
reported, because significance and effect size are different questions.

## The bug worth remembering

Wikipedia states that eight riders have won all three Grand Tours. My
pipeline returned six. Chasing the gap exposed two bugs: a fifth
annotation symbol used only on the Vuelta page, and reassigned titles
encoded as HTML rather than notation — the 2011 Vuelta produced
`"Juan José CoboChris Froome"`, two names glued together, because the
stripped rider's name sits inside an `<s>` tag that `get_text()` flattens.
Fixed by calling `decompose()` on struck elements before extracting text.

Without that external check the dataset would have looked entirely
reasonable: right row counts, plausible values, no errors raised, and two
of the sport's most decorated riders silently missing their Vuelta wins.

That is the method the project taught me: **validate outputs against
externally known facts, not just against the code.**

## Open data

The processed datasets are published in the repository so the analysis can
be reproduced without scraping or querying Wikidata. Code is MIT; the data
is CC BY-SA 4.0, because Wikipedia's share-alike terms propagate to
derived works and cannot be relicensed.
